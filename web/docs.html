How ONLY UP works — nothing hidden

Every rule here is enforced by the contract, not by us. It trades on a real price curve like any coin — but underneath sits a floor of real SOL that can only ever go up. Play with the simulator below to see the upside, your guaranteed floor, and what you keep even if the crowd dumps.

The price moves. The floor only goes up.

Two numbers matter. The curve price goes up and down as people trade, like any coin. The floor is real SOL you can always redeem your tokens for — and every buy feeds it, so the floor can only ratchet up. The contract reverts any trade that would lower it.

What happens on every trade

↑

You buy

Your SOL buys tokens on a real curve, so the price climbs as people ape in. A 3% fee comes off the top — 0.5% to the creator, 2.5% into the floor — then 20% of the rest is locked into the floor reserve, forever. The remainder drives the price, so it pumps like a normal coin.

↕

You sell

You're paid the better of the curve price or the floor — whichever is higher — minus a 3% fee (0.5% creator, 2.5% back into the floor for everyone still holding). The curve price can dip, but a sell can never drop you below the floor, and selling can never be paused.

⤴

The floor ratchets up

Every trade adds real SOL to the floor — 20% of each buy, plus 2.5% of every buy and every sell. And when someone sells, their tokens burn, so your slice of the floor grows. Reserve ÷ supply can only ever rise. The contract checks it never falls.

Everything the contract can and can't do

✓ What anyone can do

  • Buy — pay SOL, get tokens on the curve.
  • Sell — always available, never pausable, paid the better of curve or floor.
  • Donate — add SOL straight to the floor, raising it for everyone.
  • Claim creator fees — anyone can trigger it; the SOL only ever goes to the creator's set wallet.

✕ What nobody can do — not even us

  • Take SOL out of the reserves (the only payout is to sellers).
  • Mint tokens for free — new tokens only come from a paid buy.
  • Change the fees after launch. 3% a side — 0.5% creator, 2.5% floor — plus 20% of buys, all fixed in the code.
  • Freeze your wallet or block your sell. There's no freeze authority.
  • Lower the floor — every trade re-checks it can only rise.

The guards, in plain words

The floor only goes up. Every buy and sell cross-checks the new floor against the old one and reverts if it's lower. Proven monotonic across a 2,000-market, 600,000-trade stress test — zero drops.

No cheap-mint arbitrage. Nobody can buy below the floor and redeem for a profit: new tokens are always priced at the higher of the curve or the floor, so each one carries its own backing.

The reserves always cover it. After every move, the contract re-checks the vault holds the whole curve reserve, the whole floor reserve and unclaimed creator fees. Sellers can always be paid.

The seed is locked. The curve's starting SOL can never be drained below where it began, so the market can't be emptied to zero.

The contract owns the mint. It's the only thing that can create tokens, and only against a paid buy. The deployer hands this over at launch and can't take it back.

The admin is near-powerless. It can only pause buying (never selling) or point the creator fee at a new wallet — then renounce, after which it can touch nothing at all.

No keys. Nothing to rug.

The curve reserve and the floor reserve aren't wallets anybody owns. They live in a program-derived address — an account controlled only by the contract's code, with no private key anywhere in the world. Nobody can sign for it: not the team, not a hacker who steals every key we have. The only way SOL ever leaves is the contract paying a seller. There is no "dev wallet" with a hand in the reserves, because there's no key to put one there.

The honest trade-off

ONLY UP can pump like any memecoin — there's real upside. The difference is the floor underneath, which can't fall. The cost of that floor: 20% of every buy goes to the floor instead of straight into the price, so the curve climbs a touch slower than a pure pump would — but most of your buy still drives the price. You're trading a sliver of top-end speed for a bottom that can't drop out. The simulator shows real scenarios, not a promise — so you can decide if that trade is for you.
